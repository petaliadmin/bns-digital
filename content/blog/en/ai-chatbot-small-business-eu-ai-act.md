Answering the same questions ten times a day, missing requests that come in at night or at the weekend: that is daily life for many small businesses. A well-designed **AI chatbot** can take on part of that work, as long as you pick the right use cases and follow the rules, which are becoming clearer in Europe with the **AI Act**.

## What an AI chatbot does well

Today's chatbots run on language models that understand freely worded questions. Fed with **your own content** (website, FAQ, terms, catalogue), they can:

- **answer frequent questions**: opening hours, prices, lead times, service areas, order tracking;
- **qualify a request**: collect the need, budget and contact details before handing over to a person;
- **book appointments** by connecting to your calendar;
- **reply in several languages**, useful for English- and French-speaking customers;
- **stay available evenings and weekends**, when your team isn't.

## What to keep it away from

- Making **commitments** (discounts, deadlines, refunds) without human approval.
- Giving **high-stakes advice**: legal, medical, financial.
- Answering on topics **not covered by your content**: a good chatbot knows how to say "I don't know" and hand over.

Always provide an **easy route to a human**: a button, an email address, a callback.

## The AI Act: the transparency obligation

The [EU Artificial Intelligence Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32024R1689) entered into force on August 1, 2024 and applies in stages. For a customer-service chatbot, the main rule is **transparency**: people must be told they are interacting with an AI, unless it is obvious. According to the [European Commission](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai), these transparency rules take effect in **August 2026**.

In practice, a welcome message such as "I'm [your company]'s virtual assistant" is enough in most cases.

A customer-service chatbot generally falls into the "limited risk" category. The AI Act's heavier obligations target high-risk systems, such as CV screening or credit scoring.

## The GDPR applies too

A chatbot collects personal data: name, email, conversation content. For European users, the GDPR applies:

- inform users in your **privacy policy**;
- limit how long conversations are **kept**;
- check **where the data is processed** and have a contract with the provider;
- don't ask for sensitive information unless necessary.

## And in Canada?

In Quebec, [Law 25](/en/blog/quebec-law-25-website-compliance) governs the personal information your chatbot collects, including sharing it with a provider outside Quebec. If a decision is based **exclusively** on automated processing, the person must be informed. Elsewhere in Canada, PIPEDA sets the rules for personal information collected in the course of commercial activities.

## In practice

A good chatbot project starts small: one precise use case (your ten most frequent questions, for example), tested on real conversations, then expanded. Track the share of questions resolved and hand-offs to people.
