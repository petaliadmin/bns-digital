In Canada, Belgium or Switzerland, a **bilingual English-French website** is not a luxury: it is how you reach your whole market. But a poorly built bilingual site can rank worse than a site in a single language.

Here are the rules to follow.

## 1. A separate URL for each language

Each version needs **its own address**. Google [explicitly recommends this](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/specialty/international/managing-multi-regional-sites) and advises against changing the language of a single URL based on the browser or a cookie.

Three possible structures:

- **subdirectories**: `yoursite.ca/` and `yoursite.ca/fr/` (the simplest, recommended for small businesses);
- **subdomains**: `en.yoursite.ca` and `fr.yoursite.ca`;
- **separate domains**: useful for two distinct brands, but harder to rank.

Subdirectories concentrate authority on a single domain: often the best choice.

## 2. Correct hreflang tags

**Hreflang** tags tell Google that two pages are the same page in two languages. According to [Google's documentation](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/specialty/international/localized-versions):

- each page lists **all** its versions, **including itself**;
- links must be **reciprocal**: if the English page points to the French one, the French page must point back;
- you use a language code (`en`, `fr`), optionally followed by a country code (`en-CA`, `fr-CA`);
- the `x-default` value marks the default version.

A useful detail: if your content is the same for all English speakers, use `en` rather than `en-CA`. You then cover Canada, the UK and beyond in one go.

## 3. Real translation, not raw machine translation

Google detects a page's language from its **content**, and unedited machine translation shows. Beyond SEO, it is a matter of credibility: a Quebec customer immediately notices an awkward French page.

Adapt your **keywords** too: people don't necessarily search for the same thing in both languages. "Soumission" (quote), "courriel" (email) and "magasiner" (to shop) are common terms in Quebec French.

## 4. Translated URLs and metadata

Translate **addresses** (`/en/services/website-design` and `/services/creation-site-web`), **titles**, **descriptions** and image **alt text**. A French URL on an English page sends a mixed signal.

## 5. A simple language switcher, with no forced redirects

Put the switcher in the header, with each language written in that language ("English", "Français"). It should lead to **the same page** in the other language, not the home page.

Avoid automatically redirecting visitors based on their browser language: Googlebot mostly crawls from the United States and might never see your French version.

## 6. The Quebec case

Quebec's [Charter of the French language](https://www.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/) governs the use of French in business, including online. For a company selling in Quebec, a French version is not optional. Have your specific obligations checked by a lawyer or with the Office québécois de la langue française.

## In practice
