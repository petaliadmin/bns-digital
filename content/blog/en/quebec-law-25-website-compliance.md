**Quebec's Law 25** has reshaped how businesses must protect personal information. Your website is often where you collect it first: contact forms, newsletter sign-ups, analytics and advertising cookies. That also makes it the first place where a gap becomes visible.

Here is what to check on the website of a business operating in Quebec.

## Law 25 in brief

Law 25 (*An Act to modernize legislative provisions as regards the protection of personal information*) was passed in 2021. It amends, among others, the [Act respecting the protection of personal information in the private sector](https://www.legisquebec.gouv.qc.ca/en/document/cs/P-39.1), and its obligations came into force in three stages:

- **September 22, 2022**: person in charge of the protection of personal information, confidentiality incident management;
- **September 22, 2023**: most obligations (privacy policy, consent, transparency, administrative penalties);
- **September 22, 2024**: the right to data portability.

The regulator is the [Commission d'accès à l'information du Québec](https://www.cai.gouv.qc.ca/) (CAI).

## 1. Publish the title and contact details of the person in charge

Every business has a **person in charge of the protection of personal information**. By default, this is the person with the highest authority (often the president or CEO), who can delegate the role in writing.

On your website: publish their **title** and **contact details**. A dedicated email address, such as privacy@yourcompany.ca, is good practice.

## 2. A clear, accessible privacy policy

If you collect personal information by technological means, and a website form counts, you must publish a privacy policy in **clear and simple terms**. It should explain:

- what information you collect and for what purposes;
- how you collect it (forms, cookies, third-party tools);
- who has access, including service providers (hosting, newsletter tool, CRM);
- people's rights and how to exercise them.

Link to it from your footer so it is visible on every page.

## 3. Turn tracking and profiling off by default

This is the requirement that affects websites the most. If you use technology that can **identify, locate or profile** a person, you must inform them, and those functions must be **turned off by default** ([section 8.1](https://www.legisquebec.gouv.qc.ca/en/document/cs/P-39.1)).

In practice, advertising cookies, tracking pixels (Meta, LinkedIn, TikTok) and analytics tools that build a visitor profile should not fire until the visitor has agreed. A **consent banner** that blocks these tools until the visitor makes a choice meets this requirement.

## 4. Real consent on every form

Consent must be **manifest, free and enlightened**, and given for **specific purposes**. For your forms, this means:

- a newsletter checkbox that is **unticked by default** and separate from submitting the form;
- a short note under the form explaining how the data will be used, with a link to your privacy policy;
- asking only for the fields you really need.

## 5. Control third-party tools and transfers outside Quebec

Your website relies on providers: hosting, forms, CRM, newsletter, live chat. Many store data **outside Quebec**. Before communicating personal information outside Quebec, Law 25 requires a **privacy impact assessment** and a written agreement with the provider.

Make an inventory of these tools: you will need it for both your privacy policy and your assessments.

## 6. Keep, then destroy

Don't keep contact requests or inactive subscriber lists forever. Set a retention period, state it in your privacy policy, and destroy or anonymize the data once its purpose has been fulfilled.

## 7. Be ready for incidents

A hacked form or an exposed contact database is a **confidentiality incident**. You must keep a **register** of incidents and, if an incident presents a risk of serious injury, notify the CAI and the people concerned. Security updates, backups and restricted admin access all reduce the risk.

## What is at stake

Administrative monetary penalties can reach **$10 million or 2% of worldwide turnover**, and penal fines **$25 million or 4%**. The maximum amounts mainly target large companies, but a single visitor complaint or incident is enough for the CAI to step in.

## In practice

For most small-business websites, compliance comes down to a few concrete tasks: a consent banner that actually blocks cookies, an up-to-date privacy policy, reviewed forms and a named person in charge.
